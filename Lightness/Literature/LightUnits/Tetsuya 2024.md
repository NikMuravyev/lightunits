### Key points
- **object of study:** light-verb constructions with a noun “look” in English
- **context:** English has a great variety of complex-predicate constructions functionally near-equivalent to simple verbs. Such constructions feature desemantized uses of verbs called “light verbs”
- **research question:** are light verbs semantically empty? how are they compared to regular verbs?
- **data:** corpus data from COCA, constructed examples evaluated by native speakers
- **results:** LVs are neither semantically empty, nor fully regular verbs but rather a scale between those two poles. They retain three semantic components: force-dynamics, participant roles, and manner
- **noteworthy observations:**
- **interpretation:** all LVCs with “look” form a constructional network with

><mark style="background: #ffd400">The English deverbal noun look denotes an act of looking, particularly when used in conjunction with what are termed “light verbs” (Jespersen (1954)), such as take, have, and get (e.g. She {took / had / got} a look inside the room). Jespersen (1954:117-118) characterizes these verbs as “insignificant verbs” because they provide little or no semantic contribution. </mark> ([p. ](file:///home/yenotmur/snap/zotero-snap/common/Zotero/storage/2K9ZCSVA/Tetsuya%20-%202024%20-%20Can%20Heavy%20Verbs%20Be%20Light%20Verbs%20A%20Constructional%20Approach%20to%20the%20Transitive%20V%20...%20Look%20Construction.pdf))  

---

  
><mark style="background: #ffd400">An intriguing question here is what aspects must be considered when seeking to delineate between light and regular verbs. </mark> ([p. ](file:///home/yenotmur/snap/zotero-snap/common/Zotero/storage/2K9ZCSVA/Tetsuya%20-%202024%20-%20Can%20Heavy%20Verbs%20Be%20Light%20Verbs%20A%20Constructional%20Approach%20to%20the%20Transitive%20V%20...%20Look%20Construction.pdf))  

---

  
><mark style="background: #ffd400">The meaning of the light verb construction has generally been expressed with a monomorphemic paraphrase. For example, take a look is equivalent in meaning to the simple verb look, which implies that the light verb is semantically empty as it does not contribute to the overall sentence meaning that it syntactically heads (Poutsma (1926), Curme (1931, 1935), Jespersen (1954), Quirk et al. (1985), Sinclair and Renouf (1988), Dixon (1991, 2005), Huddleston and Pullum (2002), and many others). </mark> ([p. 2](file:///home/yenotmur/snap/zotero-snap/common/Zotero/storage/2K9ZCSVA/Tetsuya%20-%202024%20-%20Can%20Heavy%20Verbs%20Be%20Light%20Verbs%20A%20Constructional%20Approach%20to%20the%20Transitive%20V%20...%20Look%20Construction.pdf))  

---

  
><mark style="background: #ffd400">However, there are some cases where monomorphemic paraphrases do not replace the light verb plus its direct object (cf. Kishimoto and Okada (2020)). For example, consider (2a), where the event nominal rest is modified by well-earned. This modification is not applicable to its verbal equivalent (e.g. *rest well-earnedly is not acceptable). Again, as in (2b), have a walk and walk have different aspectual properties, as, unlike walk, have a walk can only denote an unbounded action without any explicit goal external to the agent. </mark> ([p. 2](file:///home/yenotmur/snap/zotero-snap/common/Zotero/storage/2K9ZCSVA/Tetsuya%20-%202024%20-%20Can%20Heavy%20Verbs%20Be%20Light%20Verbs%20A%20Constructional%20Approach%20to%20the%20Transitive%20V%20...%20Look%20Construction.pdf))  

---

  
><mark style="background: #ffd400">(2) a. We took a well-earned rest. (cf. *We rested well-earnedly.) (Huddleston and Pullum (2002:291)) b. He had a walk {in / *to} the park. (cf. He walked {in / to} the park.) (adapted from Huddleston and Pullum (2002:292)) </mark> ([p. 2](file:///home/yenotmur/snap/zotero-snap/common/Zotero/storage/2K9ZCSVA/Tetsuya%20-%202024%20-%20Can%20Heavy%20Verbs%20Be%20Light%20Verbs%20A%20Constructional%20Approach%20to%20the%20Transitive%20V%20...%20Look%20Construction.pdf))  

---

  
><mark style="background: #ffd400">Moreover, the passive interpretation of postverbal event nominals provides another evidence against the claim that light verb constructions are semantically equivalent to their corresponding verbal predicates:  (3) a. I had a fright. (≈ I was frightened.) b. The baby’s having a bath. (≈ The baby is being bathed.) </mark> ([p. 2](file:///home/yenotmur/snap/zotero-snap/common/Zotero/storage/2K9ZCSVA/Tetsuya%20-%202024%20-%20Can%20Heavy%20Verbs%20Be%20Light%20Verbs%20A%20Constructional%20Approach%20to%20the%20Transitive%20V%20...%20Look%20Construction.pdf))  

---

  
><mark style="background: #ffd400">This supposition is supported by recent studies that emphasize the important role light verbs play in determining the properties of the event structure and argument structure in the construction (Butt (1995, 2010), Newman (1996), Karimi (1997), Brugman (2001), Butt and Geuder (2001), Folli et al. (2005), Wittenberg et al. (2014a, b), Bruening (2016)). </mark> ([p. 3](file:///home/yenotmur/snap/zotero-snap/common/Zotero/storage/2K9ZCSVA/Tetsuya%20-%202024%20-%20Can%20Heavy%20Verbs%20Be%20Light%20Verbs%20A%20Constructional%20Approach%20to%20the%20Transitive%20V%20...%20Look%20Construction.pdf))  

---

  
><mark style="background: #ffd400">Bruening (2016) presents a more radical view by positing that light verb constructions are “just regular verb-object combinations. The verb has its usual argument structure and interpretation (which is often quite unspecified); its complement has the same interpretation it has elsewhere” (ibid.:58). </mark> ([p. 3](file:///home/yenotmur/snap/zotero-snap/common/Zotero/storage/2K9ZCSVA/Tetsuya%20-%202024%20-%20Can%20Heavy%20Verbs%20Be%20Light%20Verbs%20A%20Constructional%20Approach%20to%20the%20Transitive%20V%20...%20Look%20Construction.pdf))  

---

  
><mark style="background: #ffd400">Bruening (2016) substantiates this regular verb analysis with several empirical observations, two of which are particularly noteworthy. First, light verb constructions accommodate modifiers that create semantic conflicts, which are not feasible in corresponding simple verbs:  (4) a. The man’s wings slowly gave a quick flap. b.* The man’s wings slowly flapped quickly. (Bruening (2016:53), italics mine) </mark> ([p. 3](file:///home/yenotmur/snap/zotero-snap/common/Zotero/storage/2K9ZCSVA/Tetsuya%20-%202024%20-%20Can%20Heavy%20Verbs%20Be%20Light%20Verbs%20A%20Constructional%20Approach%20to%20the%20Transitive%20V%20...%20Look%20Construction.pdf))  

---

  
><mark style="background: #ffd400">Second, different verbs can permit different argument realization patterns. For instance, the verb give, when paired with a punch, disallows a PP and permits only an indirect object with a double object construction. However, take excludes an indirect object, and only allows a direct object plus a PP to follow:  (5) a. * She gave a punch at him. / She gave him a punch. b. She took a punch at him. / * She took him a punch. (Bruening (2016:56)) </mark> ([p. 3](file:///home/yenotmur/snap/zotero-snap/common/Zotero/storage/2K9ZCSVA/Tetsuya%20-%202024%20-%20Can%20Heavy%20Verbs%20Be%20Light%20Verbs%20A%20Constructional%20Approach%20to%20the%20Transitive%20V%20...%20Look%20Construction.pdf))  

---

  
><mark style="background: #ffd400">Bruening’s (2016) regular verb analysis provides interesting insights into the grammatical functions of light verbs: it is light verbs, rather than their complement NPs, that determine the argument structure of light verb constructions. </mark> ([p. 4](file:///home/yenotmur/snap/zotero-snap/common/Zotero/storage/2K9ZCSVA/Tetsuya%20-%202024%20-%20Can%20Heavy%20Verbs%20Be%20Light%20Verbs%20A%20Constructional%20Approach%20to%20the%20Transitive%20V%20...%20Look%20Construction.pdf))  

---

  
><mark style="background: #ffd400">An argument against Bruening’s (2016) approach is related to the unpredictability of certain event nominals that do not conform to the main verb’s selectional restrictions in the construction. Otomo (2004) notes that complement NPs of various semantic types, including a look, can be used with the verb cut to denote single events:  (6) cut a stark contrast, cut a big grin, cut a good interview, cut an impressive look, cut a warning look, cut a genuine smile, cut a swift turn, ...  (cited from Otomo (2004:237), italics mine) </mark> ([p. 4](file:///home/yenotmur/snap/zotero-snap/common/Zotero/storage/2K9ZCSVA/Tetsuya%20-%202024%20-%20Can%20Heavy%20Verbs%20Be%20Light%20Verbs%20A%20Constructional%20Approach%20to%20the%20Transitive%20V%20...%20Look%20Construction.pdf))  

---

  
><mark style="background: #ffd400">Bruening’s (2016) analysis is further challenged by the observation that light verbs have different interpretations of the directedness of the action. Brugman (2001) illustrates this with the verb take, showing that the direct objects in different constructions convey events that have different relationships between the participants:  (7) a. The bomber took a hit. b. The bomber took a reading. (Brugman (2001:567)) </mark> ([p. 4](file:///home/yenotmur/snap/zotero-snap/common/Zotero/storage/2K9ZCSVA/Tetsuya%20-%202024%20-%20Can%20Heavy%20Verbs%20Be%20Light%20Verbs%20A%20Constructional%20Approach%20to%20the%20Transitive%20V%20...%20Look%20Construction.pdf))  

---

  
><mark style="background: #ffd400">It is now evident that light verbs are distinct from their regular verb counterparts, and far from the sole determinants of the argument structure properties of the light verb construction. Instead, event nominals and prepositional complements can contribute significantly to the constructional meaning. Regarding the light-regular distinction among verbs, this study adopts a more balanced approach. </mark> ([p. 5](file:///home/yenotmur/snap/zotero-snap/common/Zotero/storage/2K9ZCSVA/Tetsuya%20-%202024%20-%20Can%20Heavy%20Verbs%20Be%20Light%20Verbs%20A%20Constructional%20Approach%20to%20the%20Transitive%20V%20...%20Look%20Construction.pdf))

><mark style="background: #ffd400">This study’s observations on transitive V ... look constructions also challenge the traditional analysis that light verbs are semantically ‘light.’ In fact, certain semantic properties of the main verb are inherited by the overall meaning of each construction type and elaborate on how the act of looking is performed. </mark> ([p. 16](file:///home/yenotmur/snap/zotero-snap/common/Zotero/storage/2K9ZCSVA/Tetsuya%20-%202024%20-%20Can%20Heavy%20Verbs%20Be%20Light%20Verbs%20A%20Constructional%20Approach%20to%20the%20Transitive%20V%20...%20Look%20Construction.pdf))  

---

  
><mark style="background: #ffd400">I propose that three semantic components carried over from the verbal semantics to the constructional semantics: (i) directedness of the action (force-dynamic properties), (ii) role of each participant, and (iii) manner of the action. </mark> ([p. 16](file:///home/yenotmur/snap/zotero-snap/common/Zotero/storage/2K9ZCSVA/Tetsuya%20-%202024%20-%20Can%20Heavy%20Verbs%20Be%20Light%20Verbs%20A%20Constructional%20Approach%20to%20the%20Transitive%20V%20...%20Look%20Construction.pdf))  

---

  
><mark style="background: #ffd400">The main verb determines the causal relationships between the participants, which corresponds to who looks at what and how the act of looking is carried out. For instance, when accompanied with the verb give in (10a), He gave a quick look at Sarah, the meaning of look can be interpreted as a centrifugal act of ‘someone looking at or toward something.’ </mark> ([p. 16](file:///home/yenotmur/snap/zotero-snap/common/Zotero/storage/2K9ZCSVA/Tetsuya%20-%202024%20-%20Can%20Heavy%20Verbs%20Be%20Light%20Verbs%20A%20Constructional%20Approach%20to%20the%20Transitive%20V%20...%20Look%20Construction.pdf))  

---

  
><mark style="background: #ffd400">In summary, the force-dynamics of the main verb determine the directedness of the act of looking, and significantly contribute to the diverse meanings of transitive V ... look constructions (cf. Newman (1996) and Brugman (2001)). </mark> ([p. 17](file:///home/yenotmur/snap/zotero-snap/common/Zotero/storage/2K9ZCSVA/Tetsuya%20-%202024%20-%20Can%20Heavy%20Verbs%20Be%20Light%20Verbs%20A%20Constructional%20Approach%20to%20the%20Transitive%20V%20...%20Look%20Construction.pdf))  

---

  
><mark style="background: #ffd400">This analysis is supported by evidence from prepositional selection. In (25), the italicized PPs cannot be attributed to the lexical properties of look because the corresponding verb, to look, is incompatible with them:  (25) a. Mary received a look from John. (cf. *Mary looked from John.) b. Mary exchanged a look with John. (cf. # Mary looked with John.) </mark> ([p. 17](file:///home/yenotmur/snap/zotero-snap/common/Zotero/storage/2K9ZCSVA/Tetsuya%20-%202024%20-%20Can%20Heavy%20Verbs%20Be%20Light%20Verbs%20A%20Constructional%20Approach%20to%20the%20Transitive%20V%20...%20Look%20Construction.pdf))  

---

  
><mark style="background: #ffd400">The selected prepositions must be compatible with the verbs’ force-dynamic properties.  (26) a. * John threw a look from Mary. b. * John received a look at Mary.  In (26), the directedness of the actions evoked by the main verbs does not fit the direction encoded by the prepositional complement. The from in (26a) does not fit with the outward-directed action of throw, whereas the at in (26b) is inconsistent with the inward-directed implication of receive. </mark> ([p. 17](file:///home/yenotmur/snap/zotero-snap/common/Zotero/storage/2K9ZCSVA/Tetsuya%20-%202024%20-%20Can%20Heavy%20Verbs%20Be%20Light%20Verbs%20A%20Constructional%20Approach%20to%20the%20Transitive%20V%20...%20Look%20Construction.pdf))  

---

  
><mark style="background: #ffd400">Another aspect of verb meaning that extends to the overall constructional meaning is the assignment of semantic roles to the subject NP, which can vary significantly depending on the verb’s argument structure. This variation is evident in the fact that give typically selects an agent as its subject, whereas receive usually selects the patient (consider the unacceptability in *John {volitionally / deliberately} received a look from Mary). </mark> ([p. 18](file:///home/yenotmur/snap/zotero-snap/common/Zotero/storage/2K9ZCSVA/Tetsuya%20-%202024%20-%20Can%20Heavy%20Verbs%20Be%20Light%20Verbs%20A%20Constructional%20Approach%20to%20the%20Transitive%20V%20...%20Look%20Construction.pdf))  

---

  
><mark style="background: #ffd400">Third, as discussed in Section 4.1.3, the main verb in the transitive V ... look construction can contribute a manner component to the constructional meaning. For example, in Otomo’s (2004) example cut a warning look in (6), the movement of the subject’s gaze is depicted as a cutting-like, quick motion. Similarly, in (14b), Marjorie stole a look at her watch, the verb steal provides the manner meaning with the looking event, which results in the constructional meaning of ‘secretly looking at something.’ </mark> ([p. 18](file:///home/yenotmur/snap/zotero-snap/common/Zotero/storage/2K9ZCSVA/Tetsuya%20-%202024%20-%20Can%20Heavy%20Verbs%20Be%20Light%20Verbs%20A%20Constructional%20Approach%20to%20the%20Transitive%20V%20...%20Look%20Construction.pdf))  

---

  
><mark style="background: #ffd400">In conclusion, the main verbs in the transitive V ... look constructions are not entirely devoid of semantic predicational content. Rather, the overall constructional meaning incorporates various semantic properties from the main verbs, including their force-dynamic properties, semantic roles assigned to the event participants, and the manners of the action. </mark> ([p. 18](file:///home/yenotmur/snap/zotero-snap/common/Zotero/storage/2K9ZCSVA/Tetsuya%20-%202024%20-%20Can%20Heavy%20Verbs%20Be%20Light%20Verbs%20A%20Constructional%20Approach%20to%20the%20Transitive%20V%20...%20Look%20Construction.pdf))  

---

  
><mark style="background: #ffd400">As Huddleston and Pullum (2002) point out, the main semantic content in the construction resides in look. This is demonstrated by the selection of the verb as a fragment question in (27), where the meaning of the V ... look construction can only be paraphrased using the verb form look, rather than the main verb: </mark> ([p. 18](file:///home/yenotmur/snap/zotero-snap/common/Zotero/storage/2K9ZCSVA/Tetsuya%20-%202024%20-%20Can%20Heavy%20Verbs%20Be%20Light%20Verbs%20A%20Constructional%20Approach%20to%20the%20Transitive%20V%20...%20Look%20Construction.pdf))  

---

  
><mark style="background: #ffd400">(27) A: John {got / stole / threw} a look at it. B: What did he do? He {looked / *got / *stole / *threw}? A: Yes. </mark> ([p. 19](file:///home/yenotmur/snap/zotero-snap/common/Zotero/storage/2K9ZCSVA/Tetsuya%20-%202024%20-%20Can%20Heavy%20Verbs%20Be%20Light%20Verbs%20A%20Constructional%20Approach%20to%20the%20Transitive%20V%20...%20Look%20Construction.pdf))  

---

  
><mark style="background: #ffd400">This consideration extends to preposition selection. As shown in (10a), the perceived object must be marked by at, which is selected by look, rather than by the preposition chosen by the verb:  (28) a. John gave a look {at / *to} her. (cf. John gave money {*at / to} her.) b. John stole a look {at / *from} her. (cf. John stole money {*at / from} her.) </mark> ([p. 19](file:///home/yenotmur/snap/zotero-snap/common/Zotero/storage/2K9ZCSVA/Tetsuya%20-%202024%20-%20Can%20Heavy%20Verbs%20Be%20Light%20Verbs%20A%20Constructional%20Approach%20to%20the%20Transitive%20V%20...%20Look%20Construction.pdf))  

---

  
><mark style="background: #ffd400">I propose that the semantic predication of the transitive V ... look construction can be explained by adopting the concept of “constructions” as proposed in Construction Grammar, i.e. particular form-meaning pairings. To be more specific, it is necessary to posit the existence of constructional schemas, such as [NP VERB ... look at NP] for Active Visual Perception, [NP VERB ... look from NP] for Passive Visual Perception, and others. </mark> ([p. 19](file:///home/yenotmur/snap/zotero-snap/common/Zotero/storage/2K9ZCSVA/Tetsuya%20-%202024%20-%20Can%20Heavy%20Verbs%20Be%20Light%20Verbs%20A%20Constructional%20Approach%20to%20the%20Transitive%20V%20...%20Look%20Construction.pdf))
